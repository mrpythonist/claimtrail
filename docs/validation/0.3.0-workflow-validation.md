# ClaimTrail v0.3.0: Workflow Validation

Phase 0, Business Validation. Written 2026-11-22.

This document describes the shipment-to-claim workflow as it works in
the real world, defines the claim lifecycle, the evidence each event
type needs, how deadlines work, and the financial terms the product
uses. Every statement about what the ClaimTrail app does was checked
against the code in `~/workspace/claimtrail/backend` and is cited with
a file path. Every statement about real-world practice comes from a
public source listed in §9, accessed 2026-11-22, and is labelled
**[sourced]**. Figures and practices that are ClaimTrail's own
reasoning are labelled **[hypothesis]** or **[estimate]**. Nothing in
this document is based on customer interviews: no customer has been
spoken to yet. That is v0.2.0's job, and §6 lists the questions only
interviews can answer.

This document builds on v0.1.0 (market research,
`0.1.0-market-research.md`). It uses the same rate ranges and the same
segment language. Where a newer source gives a wider range than
v0.1.0, the difference is flagged in the text rather than silently
replacing the v0.1.0 figure.

## 1. The shipment-to-claim workflow

A claim is the end of a chain that starts when a load is booked. Each
stage below states what happens in the real world, what the app
records, and where the two diverge. The divergences are collected in
§7.

### 1.1 Stage by stage

**Stage 1: Load booked.** The broker offers a load, the carrier or
dispatch agency accepts it, and the broker issues a rate confirmation.
The rate confirmation is the per-load contract: it names the broker
and carrier, the load number, pickup and delivery addresses and
appointment times, the commodity and weight, the equipment, the agreed
line-haul rate, payment terms, and the accessorial terms (detention
rate and free time, TONU amount, lumper authorization, layover rate)
**[sourced]**. There is no federal form for a rate confirmation; each
broker writes its own **[sourced]**. The single most important fact
about this stage: an accessorial charge that is not written on the
signed rate confirmation is, in practice, not enforceable. Trade
guides put it bluntly: if it is not on the rate con, it does not
exist **[sourced]**.

In the app, this stage is a shipment record
(`backend/app/Models/Shipment.php`): broker or customer name, internal
and external load references, pickup and delivery locations and
appointment times, base rate, currency, driver name and contact. The
rate confirmation itself is stored as evidence on the claim later, in
the `rate_confirmation` document category
(`backend/app/EvidenceDocumentCategory.php`). The app does not parse a
rate confirmation, extract its accessorial terms, or warn when a rate
confirmation has no detention clause. That is a gap (§7).

**Stage 2: Pickup.** The driver arrives at the shipper, checks in,
waits for loading, and departs. The clock that matters for detention
starts at check-in (or at the appointment time, if the driver was on
time), not when a dock door is assigned **[sourced]**. Free time is
the grace period before detention accrues. The industry standard is 2
hours of free time, with some rate confirmations specifying 1 or 3
hours **[sourced]**; ATRI found 67% of carriers use 2 hours (v0.1.0,
§2).

In the app, a shipment moves from `planned` to `in_transit`
(`backend/app/ShipmentStatus.php`), and the shipment record carries
`actual_arrival_at` and `actual_departure_at` fields
(`backend/app/Models/Shipment.php`). Those fields are manual entries.
The app has no GPS capture, no ELD link, and no check-in timestamp
from any device. Arrival and departure times are whatever a person
types in. That is a gap (§7).

**Stage 3: Transit.** The truck drives to the receiver. Delays on the
road (traffic, a prior detention, a breakdown) can push the delivery
appointment, and a missed appointment window can itself become a
dispute. In the real world the driver or dispatcher is expected to
tell the broker about a late arrival in writing as soon as it is
known **[sourced]**.

In the app, transit is the `in_transit` shipment status. There is no
transit tracking, no ETA field, and no late-arrival notice. A shipment
event can be recorded at any point, but nothing in the app models a
road delay as its own event type.

**Stage 4: Delivery.** The driver arrives at the receiver, checks in,
waits for unloading, and gets the delivery signed. The signed proof of
delivery (POD) is the closing document of the load: it records who
received the goods, when, and in what condition **[sourced]**. In
practice the signed delivery copy of the bill of lading (BOL) often
doubles as the POD **[sourced]**. The in and out times written on the
BOL or POD are the strongest detention evidence a carrier has
**[sourced]**.

In the app, the shipment moves from `in_transit` to `delivered` and
then to `completed` (`backend/app/ShipmentStatus.php`). The POD is
stored as evidence in the `proof_of_delivery` category. The app does
not check that a POD exists before a shipment is marked delivered, and
it does not require in/out times on the POD. That is a gap (§7).

**Stage 5: Event capture.** Whenever something billable happens, a
shipment event is recorded: detention at pickup or delivery, a forced
overnight layover, a cancelled load (TONU), a lumper payment, an extra
stop, extra miles, or anything else. The event is the raw material a
claim is built from. In the real world the evidence for the event is
created at the moment it happens: a timestamped photo of the gate or
check-in screen, a gate slip, a signed BOL with in/out times, a
lumper receipt, a text or email to the broker while still on site
**[sourced]**. Evidence created days later is weaker, and brokers
dispute it with their own records. ATRI's own finding, quoted in
v0.1.0, is that the shipper usually holds the time record.

In the app, an event is a `ShipmentEvent` record
(`backend/app/Models/ShipmentEvent.php`): event category, event date,
start time, end time, duration in minutes, location, description,
estimated amount, currency, notes, and who reported it. Events start
in status `potential` and can move to `under_review` or `dismissed`
(`backend/app/ShipmentEventStatus.php`); the enum's own comment is
explicit that no event status means the event qualifies for
compensation. Event categories are the seven system categories seeded
for every organization: detention, layover, TONU, lumper, additional
stop, additional miles, other
(`backend/database/seeders/EventCategorySeeder.php`). Organizations can
add their own categories (`backend/app/Models/EventCategory.php`).
Everything about an event is entered by hand. There is no same-day
capture prompt, no photo timestamp check, and no link between an
event's start/end times and the shipment's `actual_arrival_at` /
`actual_departure_at` fields.

**Stage 6: Claim assembly.** After delivery, the office (or the
dispatch agency) turns one or more events into a claim. In the real
world this means: pick the event, work out the amount from the rate
confirmation (billable hours times the hourly rate, or the flat fee),
gather the evidence, and write the claim up as an itemized invoice
with the proof attached **[sourced]**. Trade guidance is to submit
accessorial claims with the regular invoice and POD, not separately,
so the broker's accounting department processes one package
**[sourced]**.

In the app, a claim is created manually through the API
(`backend/app/Http/Controllers/Api/V1/ClaimController.php`, `store`
method). A claim requires a shipment, a requested amount, and a
currency; it may link one shipment event, and its category is inherited
from that event when no category is given
(`backend/app/ClaimCategory.php`). The claim gets a reference like
`CLM-YYYY-NNNN` from `ClaimReferenceService` and starts in status
`potential` (`backend/app/Models/Claim.php`, default status
`potential`; `database/migrations/2026_11_16_220200_create_claims_table.php`).
The app does not calculate the amount from the event times and the
rate confirmation rate; the requested amount is typed in by the user.
The app does not create a claim automatically when an event is
recorded. One claim links to at most one event, so a load with two
detention events (pickup and delivery) needs two claims or one claim
with the second event left unlinked. That is a gap (§7).

**Stage 7: Review.** Before anything goes to the broker, someone in
the organization checks the claim: is the amount right, is the
evidence complete, does the rate confirmation actually allow this
charge? In the real world this is the step that catches the claims
that would be rejected for a missing signature or a wrong rate, and it
is also where a reviewer decides a claim is not worth sending (for
example, to protect a broker relationship).

In the app, review is the `under_review` status plus the readiness
check. Readiness counts required evidence categories against attached
evidence files and reports `not_started`, `partial`, or `ready`
(`backend/app/Services/ReadinessService.php`,
`backend/app/ReadinessStatus.php`). Readiness is informational only:
the code and the enum both state it never certifies that a claim is
valid or will be accepted. Moving a claim to `ready_to_submit` freezes
a readiness snapshot, so the review decision is judged against the
evidence attached at that moment
(`backend/app/Services/ClaimTransitionService.php`). A reviewer can
override a readiness gap, but only with a mandatory written reason,
and the override is written into the claim history
(`backend/app/Services/ReadinessService.php`, `override` method).
Review notes are stored on the claim
(`backend/app/Models/ClaimReviewNote.php`). What the app does not do
is enforce readiness: a claim can move from `under_review` to
`ready_to_submit` with evidence missing, and nothing blocks it. That
is a deliberate design choice in the code, not an oversight, but it
means readiness is a guide, not a gate.

**Stage 8: Submission.** The claim goes to the broker. In the real
world this is usually an email to the billing address on the rate
confirmation, or a submission through the broker's portal, with the
itemized invoice and the proof attached **[sourced]**. Speed matters:
trade guides advise submitting within 24 to 48 hours of delivery, and
note that some brokers deny detention when the claim is filed late,
sometimes on a 24 to 72 hour deadline **[sourced]**. The contractual
submission window in a broker-carrier agreement is commonly 30 to 90
days after delivery **[sourced]** (see §4 for the full picture).

In the app, submission is a status change to `submitted`, made by a
user through the API. `submitted_at` is stamped at that moment (or a
backdated value can be supplied)
(`backend/app/Services/ClaimTransitionService.php`). The app generates
a PDF claim package: claim and shipment details, the event, an index
of the evidence files, and the status timeline
(`backend/app/Services/ClaimPackageService.php`). The evidence
originals are indexed, not embedded, and the package carries a
disclaimer that it is a supporting summary, not a guarantee of
acceptance. Critically, the app does not send the package anywhere.
There is no email to the broker, no portal submission, no delivery
confirmation. "Submitted" in ClaimTrail means "a person in the
organization recorded that this claim was submitted", not "the broker
received it". That is the largest gap in the workflow (§7).

**Stage 9: Broker response.** The broker accepts the claim, accepts
part of it, rejects it, or ignores it. In the real world, partial
acceptance is common: the broker agrees to fewer hours than claimed,
or a lower rate, or pays the lumper but not the detention. Rejection
usually comes with a reason: no detention clause on the rate con, late
filing, weak documentation, or the broker's own time record
disagreeing **[sourced]**. Silence is also a common outcome; trade
guides advise following up at 48 to 72 hours if unpaid, then
escalating to broker management, and eventually to a claim against the
broker's $75,000 FMCSA surety bond for broader nonpayment
**[sourced]**. Fewer than 50% of billed detention charges are actually
paid (v0.1.0, §1.1), so a claim that is accepted on paper is still
only halfway to cash.

In the app, the response is recorded by moving the claim from
`submitted` to `accepted`, `partially_accepted`, or `rejected`
(`backend/app/ClaimStatus.php`). Acceptance requires an accepted
amount greater than zero; partial acceptance requires the accepted
amount to be less than the requested amount; rejection requires a
written reason (`backend/app/Services/ClaimTransitionService.php`).
A rejected claim can go back to `under_review` (for rework and
resubmission) or to `closed`. There is no follow-up schedule after
submission, no escalation path, and no record of broker
communications beyond manually uploaded `broker_communication`
evidence files. The app does not track that a broker has gone silent.

**Stage 10: Payment and closure.** The broker pays, usually on the
payment terms in the rate confirmation (commonly net 30 after receipt
of invoice and POD **[sourced]**). The carrier records the payment,
reconciles it against the accepted amount, and closes the claim. In
the real world the amount actually received is often less than the
amount accepted, because of quick-pay discounts, factoring fees, or
short payment, and the difference is a real number the business needs
to see.

In the app, payment is recorded through a dedicated endpoint, not a
status change. Only an `accepted` or `partially_accepted` claim can be
marked paid, and recording a payment requires a recovered amount
greater than zero and a payment date
(`backend/app/Services/ClaimTransitionService.php`, `recordPayment`
method). The recovered amount is stored separately from the accepted
amount and is never defaulted, so the gap between accepted and
recovered is visible on the claim record. A paid claim can move to
`closed`; `closed` is terminal (`backend/app/ClaimStatus.php`). There
is no invoice reconciliation, no factoring integration, and no
accounting export for payments.

### 1.2 Workflow summary

| Stage | Real-world action | App record | App status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Load booked | Rate confirmation signed | Shipment | `draft` / `planned` |
| 2. Pickup | Check in, load, depart | Shipment arrival/departure fields | `planned` to `in_transit` |
| 3. Transit | Drive, report delays | None | `in_transit` |
| 4. Delivery | Unload, signed POD | Shipment | `in_transit` to `delivered` to `completed` |
| 5. Event capture | Record the billable event with proof | ShipmentEvent | `potential` / `under_review` / `dismissed` |
| 6. Claim assembly | Work out the amount, gather proof | Claim | `potential` |
| 7. Review | Check amount and evidence | Readiness check, review notes | `evidence_required` / `under_review` / `ready_to_submit` |
| 8. Submission | Send invoice and proof to broker | PDF claim package generated | `submitted` |
| 9. Broker response | Accept, partially accept, reject, ignore | Status change with amount/reason | `accepted` / `partially_accepted` / `rejected` |
| 10. Payment/closure | Broker pays, reconcile, close | Payment recorded | `paid` / `closed` |

## 2. Claim lifecycle

### 2.1 Statuses

The claim lifecycle is defined in `backend/app/ClaimStatus.php`
(10 statuses) and enforced by
`backend/app/Services/ClaimTransitionService.php`. Controllers never
assign a claim status directly; every change goes through the service,
which checks the transition map, applies the data rules, stamps the
lifecycle timestamps, and writes one append-only history row, all in
one transaction. Each transition also writes one audit row and
notifies organization members about outcomes that need action.

| Status | Meaning | How the claim gets there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `potential` | A possible claim, just created | Claim creation (default status) |
| `evidence_required` | Evidence is missing and someone flagged it | Manual transition from `potential` or `under_review` |
| `under_review` | Being checked inside the organization | Manual transition from `potential`, `evidence_required`, `ready_to_submit`, or `rejected` |
| `ready_to_submit` | Reviewed, evidence snapshot frozen | Manual transition from `under_review` |
| `submitted` | Recorded as sent to the broker | Manual transition from `ready_to_submit`; `submitted_at` stamped |
| `accepted` | Broker accepted the full requested amount | Manual transition from `submitted`; accepted amount required |
| `partially_accepted` | Broker accepted less than requested | Manual transition from `submitted`; accepted amount required, must be less than requested |
| `rejected` | Broker rejected the claim | Manual transition from `submitted`; written reason required |
| `paid` | Payment recorded | Only through the record-payment endpoint, from `accepted` or `partially_accepted`; recovered amount and payment date required |
| `closed` | Finished, terminal | Manual transition from most pre-submission statuses, from `rejected`, or from `paid` |

### 2.2 Allowed transitions

This table is a direct transcription of the `allowedTransitions` map
in `backend/app/ClaimStatus.php`. `paid` never appears as a target
here because it is reachable only through the record-payment endpoint.

| From | Can move to |
| --- | --- |
| `potential` | `evidence_required`, `under_review`, `closed` |
| `evidence_required` | `under_review`, `closed` |
| `under_review` | `ready_to_submit`, `evidence_required`, `closed` |
| `ready_to_submit` | `submitted`, `under_review`, `closed` |
| `submitted` | `accepted`, `partially_accepted`, `rejected` |
| `accepted` | (none; payment via record-payment endpoint only) |
| `partially_accepted` | (none; payment via record-payment endpoint only) |
| `rejected` | `closed`, `under_review` |
| `paid` | `closed` |
| `closed` | (none; terminal) |

### 2.3 Rules the lifecycle enforces

- **Payment is separate from acceptance.** An accepted claim is not
  recovered revenue until a payment is recorded against it. The code
  comment in `ClaimStatus.php` says this explicitly, and the
  transition service refuses a direct move to `paid`.
- **Accepted and partially accepted are dead ends in the map.** Once a
  claim is accepted, the only way forward is recording a payment. If
  the broker accepted the claim and then never pays, the claim sits in
  `accepted` with no follow-up state and no escalation. That is a gap
  (§7).
- **Rejection needs a reason, and rejection is not final.** A rejected
  claim can return to `under_review` for rework, which matches the
  real world, where a rejected claim is often resubmitted with better
  proof. The reason is stored on the claim (`rejection_reason`) and
  the rejection is timestamped (`rejected_at`).
- **Closing before submission needs a reason.** Closing a
  pre-submission claim (for example, deciding not to bill a broker to
  protect the relationship) requires a written reason
  (`ClaimTransitionService.php`, `transitionUpdates`). This matters
  because v0.1.0 identified "never billed on purpose" as one of the
  four leaks: the reason field at least makes the decision visible.
- **Submitted is a one-way door for the amount.** After submission the
  claim can only be accepted, partially accepted, or rejected. It
  cannot go back to review to fix a mistake; the only route is
  rejection followed by rework. In the real world a carrier would
  simply send a corrected invoice, so this is stricter than practice.
- **History is append-only.** Every transition writes a
  `ClaimStatusHistory` row with the from and to statuses, who changed
  it, the reason, and the amounts in effect at the time
  (`backend/app/Models/ClaimStatusHistory.php`). The claim package PDF
  includes this timeline.

### 2.4 How the app lifecycle differs from the real-world lifecycle

In the real world, the broker is an active party: the broker receives
the submission, asks questions, counters, pays late, or goes silent.
In the app, the broker is not a party at all. Every post-submission
state is entered by someone in the carrier's own organization, based
on what the broker told them outside the app. There is no "awaiting
broker response" timer, no counter-offer state, and no "payment
overdue" state. The lifecycle models the carrier's paperwork, not the
negotiation. That is an honest scope limit, but it should not be
mistaken for a full model of the claim's life.

## 3. Evidence requirements per event type

### 3.1 How the app handles evidence requirements

Evidence in the app has two layers:

1. **Document categories.** Ten categories exist in
   `backend/app/EvidenceDocumentCategory.php`: bill of lading, proof
   of delivery, rate confirmation, arrival and departure record,
   appointment confirmation, broker communication, driver photograph,
   receipt, lumper receipt, other. An evidence file is uploaded
   against a claim with one of these categories
   (`backend/app/Models/EvidenceFile.php`).
2. **Requirements.** An organization configures which categories are
   required, globally or per event category, in
   `EvidenceRequirement` rows
   (`backend/app/Models/EvidenceRequirement.php`). Readiness is then
   computed per claim: global requirements plus the requirements
   matching the claim event's category
   (`backend/app/Services/ReadinessService.php`).

Two facts about this design matter for validation:

- **The app ships no default requirements.** No seeder creates
  `EvidenceRequirement` rows, and no default set is defined in code.
  An organization that never configures requirements has zero
  required categories, and the readiness computation returns 100% and
  status `ready` for a claim with no evidence at all
  (`ReadinessService.php`: total zero means ready). A new organization
  therefore sees every claim as "ready" until someone configures the
  requirements. That is a gap (§7).
- **Readiness counts categories, not quality.** A claim is "ready"
  when one file exists in each required category. The app does not
  check that the BOL has in/out times written on it, that the photo is
  timestamped, or that the rate confirmation actually contains a
  detention clause. A complete-looking claim can still be a weak
  claim. The code says this plainly in its disclaimer, and this
  document repeats it: readiness is a checklist, not a judgement.

### 3.2 Recommended evidence matrix

The matrix below is ClaimTrail's recommended default set of required
and recommended evidence per event type. It is labelled
**[hypothesis]** throughout: it is assembled from the trade sources
cited in §9 and from the app's own category list, and it has not been
confirmed with any broker or carrier. It is the starting point for
v0.2.0 interviews, not a settled standard. If the app later seeds
default requirements, this matrix is the draft to test.

"Required" means the claim should not be marked ready to submit
without it. "Recommended" means it strengthens the claim and should be
attached when it exists.

| Event type | Required [hypothesis] | Recommended [hypothesis] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Detention | Rate confirmation (shows free time and hourly rate); arrival and departure record (check-in and out times); bill of lading or proof of delivery with in/out times | Driver photo of gate/check-in screen; broker communication showing notice sent at free-time expiry; appointment confirmation |
| Layover | Rate confirmation (shows layover rate); arrival and departure record (shows the overnight hold); broker communication (shows the broker knew the truck was held) | Bill of lading or proof of delivery; appointment confirmation for the next-day appointment; driver photo |
| TONU | Rate confirmation (shows TONU amount); broker communication (the cancellation message with its time) | Arrival and departure record (proves the truck was dispatched or had arrived); receipt for deadhead or other costs incurred |
| Lumper | Lumper receipt (the paid receipt with the load or PO number) **[sourced]**; rate confirmation (shows lumper authorization or reimbursement term) | Proof of delivery; bill of lading; receipt for any related payment |
| Additional stop | Rate confirmation (shows stop-off pay); proof of delivery or bill of lading for the extra stop | Broker communication confirming the extra stop was requested; arrival and departure record for the stop |
| Additional miles | Rate confirmation (shows the agreed route or mileage); broker communication (shows the reroute or extra miles were authorized) | Arrival and departure record; driver photo of odometer or routing, if available |
| Other (custom) | Rate confirmation; broker communication | Whatever document proves the event happened and the amount |

### 3.3 What each document must prove

- **Rate confirmation.** Proves the charge was agreed before the
  truck rolled, and states the rate and the trigger. Without it, the
  broker has no contractual obligation to pay **[sourced]**. It is the
  only document that proves the right to bill, which is why it is
  required for every event type in the matrix above.
- **Bill of lading.** Proves what freight was tendered and when the
  carrier took possession. Issued at pickup; travels with the freight
  **[sourced]**. For detention claims, the in and out times written on
  the BOL are the core time evidence **[sourced]**.
- **Proof of delivery.** Proves the goods reached the intended
  recipient, who received them, when, and in what condition
  **[sourced]**. The signed delivery copy of the BOL often doubles as
  the POD **[sourced]**, which is why the app keeps them as separate
  categories but a single uploaded file can only carry one category.
  That is a small gap (§7).
- **Arrival and departure record.** Proves the two timestamps the
  detention math depends on. In the real world this is a gate slip, a
  check-in receipt, an ELD log, or a timestamped photo **[sourced]**.
  The app stores it as a file upload; it does not capture ELD or GPS
  data itself.
- **Appointment confirmation.** Proves the appointment time the free
  time clock is measured against. Free time can start at the
  appointment time or at actual check-in, depending on the rate
  confirmation **[sourced]**, so the appointment record matters when
  the two differ.
- **Broker communication.** Proves the broker was notified while the
  event was happening. Many brokers require notice while the driver is
  still on site, often before free time expires **[sourced]**. A text
  or email thread is a timestamped record the broker received and
  cannot easily dispute **[sourced]**.
- **Driver photograph.** Proves presence at a time and place: a photo
  of the gate time display, the check-in kiosk, or the dock
  **[sourced]**. Photos are only useful if they carry a trustworthy
  timestamp, which the app does not verify.
- **Receipt / lumper receipt.** Proves money was actually paid out of
  pocket. For lumper fees, the receipt is the whole claim: standard
  practice is 100% reimbursement with a receipt **[sourced]**, and
  federal law (49 U.S.C. 14103) puts the cost of required unloading
  help on the shipper or receiver **[sourced]**.

### 3.4 Evidence that the app cannot yet represent

- **ELD or GPS records.** The strongest time evidence in the real
  world **[sourced]**. The app has no ELD or GPS import; an ELD
  export would have to be uploaded as an `other` or
  `arrival_departure_record` file.
- **A single file serving two categories.** A signed BOL that is also
  the POD is one file with two roles. The app forces one category per
  file, so the same document must be uploaded twice or one role goes
  unrecorded.
- **Evidence attached to an event rather than a claim.** In the app,
  evidence files attach to claims, not to events
  (`EvidenceFile` belongs to a claim). An event recorded days before
  its claim exists has nowhere to hold its proof in the meantime. The
  driver photograph taken at the gate has to wait for the claim to be
  created before it can be filed.

## 4. Deadline handling

### 4.1 What a deadline is in the real world

There is no single statutory deadline for submitting an accessorial
claim on the road side. Whatever windows exist are contractual
**[sourced]**. The numbers that recur in trade guidance are:

- **24 to 48 hours after delivery** to submit the itemized invoice
  with proof attached **[sourced]**. This is the working norm, not a
  legal rule.
- **24 to 72 hours** as the short filing window some brokers enforce
  for detention specifically; claims filed after it are denied
  **[sourced]**.
- **30 to 90 days after delivery** as the claims submission deadline
  commonly written into broker-carrier agreements **[sourced]**.
- **30 days** as a reasonable submission window in the view of one
  practitioner summary, which notes that road freight has no
  equivalent of the ocean 30-day invoicing rule **[sourced]**.
- **Net 30** as the common payment term after receipt of invoice and
  POD **[sourced]**, which is a payment deadline, not a submission
  deadline, and should not be confused with one.

The honest summary is that the binding deadline is whatever the rate
confirmation or the broker-carrier agreement says, and it varies by
broker. A product that assumes one fixed window will be wrong for a
large share of its users. That is why ClaimTrail makes the window
configurable per organization, and why v0.2.0 must ask carriers what
windows their brokers actually enforce.

### 4.2 How the app handles deadlines

Deadlines are created at claim creation. If the organization has
configured a deadline setting, and the claim was created without an
explicit deadline, the app creates a `ClaimDeadline` row with the
deadline set to now plus the configured number of days, in the
organization's timezone
(`backend/app/Http/Controllers/Api/V1/ClaimController.php`,
`applyDeadlineDefault`). An explicit `submission_deadline_at` supplied
at creation always wins over the default. The claim record also stores
`submission_deadline_at` as a denormalized copy.

**Scope precedence.** When several settings could apply, the most
specific one wins: a setting scoped to the claim category first, then
one scoped to the event category, then the global setting
(`backend/app/Models/DeadlineSetting.php`,
`backend/app/Services/CustomFieldService.php`,
`resolveDeadlineSetting`). A setting has `submit_within_days` (1 to
365, enforced by controller validation) and `reminder_days_before` (a
list of day counts). If the organization configured no setting at all,
no deadline row is created and the claim has no deadline. That is a
gap (§7): a claim can exist with no submission deadline and nothing
warns anyone.

**Deadline states.** A deadline's status is recomputed by the
`claimtrail:refresh-deadline-statuses` command, scheduled hourly
(`backend/routes/console.php`), implemented in
`backend/app/Services/DeadlineStatusService.php` and defined in
`backend/app/DeadlineStatus.php`. The rules, in order:

| State | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `upcoming` | Deadline is in the future, outside the reminder window |
| `due_soon` | Deadline is inside the reminder window: the largest configured reminder value, or 7 days when none are set (`backend/app/Models/ClaimDeadline.php`, `reminderWindowDays`) |
| `overdue` | Deadline passed and the claim has not been submitted |
| `met` | The claim was submitted on or before the deadline |
| `missed` | The claim was submitted after the deadline, or the claim was closed without submission after the deadline passed |

Comparisons use the deadline's own timezone, so a 5pm Chicago deadline
is judged at 5pm Chicago time, not UTC
(`DeadlineStatusService.php`). Only active (non-superseded) deadlines
are refreshed. Due-soon and overdue deadlines notify the organization's
members, at most once per deadline per day (dedupe key per deadline
per day). Met, missed, and far-future upcoming deadlines stay quiet.

**What the deadline measures.** The deadline is measured from the
moment the claim is created, not from the delivery date or the event
date (`applyDeadlineDefault` uses `now()` plus the configured days).
A claim created five days after delivery with a 30-day setting gets a
deadline 30 days from creation, which is 35 days after delivery. The
real-world clock runs from delivery. That is a gap (§7).

**What deadlines do not do.** The app tracks submission deadlines
only. It does not track payment deadlines (net 30 after submission),
broker response deadlines, or follow-up schedules. There is no
escalation when a deadline is missed: the status flips to `overdue`
or `missed`, a notification goes out once a day, and nothing else
happens. The app never submits anything automatically, so a missed
deadline is a lost claim, not a delayed one.

### 4.3 Deadline handling summary

| Question | Answer in the app |
| --- | --- |
| When is a deadline created? | At claim creation, from the organization default, unless an explicit deadline was supplied |
| What is the window measured from? | Claim creation time, not delivery or event date |
| Can the window vary by claim type? | Yes: claim category, then event category, then global |
| What if no setting exists? | No deadline row is created; the claim has no deadline |
| How often are statuses recomputed? | Hourly, by scheduled command |
| What reminders exist? | Due-soon and overdue notifications, once per deadline per day |
| What happens when a deadline is missed? | Status becomes `overdue` or `missed`; notification only; no escalation, no auto-submission |

## 5. Financial terminology

This glossary defines the terms ClaimTrail uses, in the sense the
trucking industry uses them, with rate ranges carried over from v0.1.0
unless flagged. Rates are market conventions from trade sources, not
law; the rate confirmation on a given load governs. Where a newer
2026 trade guide gives a wider range than v0.1.0, the wider range is
noted and flagged for interview testing.

| Term | Meaning | Typical rate [sourced] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Accessorial charge** | Any fee beyond the base line-haul rate, for time, equipment, special handling, or difficult locations **[sourced]**. Detention, layover, TONU, and lumper are all accessorials. | Varies by charge |
| **Detention** | Pay for waiting at a shipper or receiver beyond the agreed free time, usually 2 hours **[sourced]**. Billed hourly after free time expires. The most common truckload accessorial and the largest single leakage pool (v0.1.0). | $50-$75/hr, ATRI average charged ~$63/hr, range $25-$100 (v0.1.0). Newer 2026 trade guides cite $50-$100/hr **[sourced]**; the difference is flagged, not resolved, pending interviews. |
| **Free time** | The grace period after arrival during which no detention accrues. Standard is 2 hours; 67% of carriers use 2 hours (ATRI, via v0.1.0). Some rate confirmations specify 1 or 3 hours **[sourced]**. The clock usually starts at check-in or appointment time, per the rate confirmation **[sourced]**. | 2 hours (standard) |
| **Layover** | Pay when a delay forces an overnight wait to the next day, through no fault of the carrier **[sourced]**. Billed per day or per night. | $250-$350/day, range $150-$500 (v0.1.0). Newer guides cite $150-$300/night **[sourced]**; flagged, pending interviews. |
| **TONU (Truck Ordered Not Used)** | Pay when a load is cancelled after the truck was dispatched or had arrived. Also called a dry run fee **[sourced]**. Compensates the dispatch and any deadhead miles driven. | $200-$300, range $150-$600 (v0.1.0). Some guides scale it by stage: $0-$50 before departure, $100-$200 en route, $200-$400 after arrival **[sourced]**; flagged, pending interviews. |
| **Lumper fee** | A fee paid to a third-party crew for loading or unloading at a warehouse or distribution center, common at grocery and big-retail DCs **[sourced]**. Usually paid by the driver at the dock against a receipt, then reimbursed **[sourced]**. Under 49 U.S.C. 14103, a shipper or receiver that requires unloading help must provide it or pay for it **[sourced]**. | $150-$250, range $50-$350 (v0.1.0). Other guides cite $25-$500 per load **[sourced]**; flagged, pending interviews. |
| **Additional stop (stop-off)** | Pay for each delivery or pickup stop beyond the first one on a load **[sourced]**. | $50-$200 each (v0.1.0) |
| **Additional miles** | Pay for miles driven beyond the agreed route, for example after a reroute **[hypothesis]**. No standard rate found in the sources consulted; the rate confirmation governs. | Per rate confirmation |
| **Driver assist** | Pay when the driver is required to help load, unload, or sort freight **[sourced]**. Not a first-class event type in ClaimTrail; handled as a custom event. | $50-$150 (v0.1.0) |
| **Line haul** | The base rate for moving the freight from origin to destination, before fuel surcharge and accessorials **[sourced]**. | Per load |
| **Fuel surcharge (FSC)** | A fuel amount listed separately from line haul, or included in an all-in rate **[sourced]**. | Per rate confirmation |
| **Rate confirmation (rate con)** | The per-load contract between broker and carrier: parties, load number, stops, commodity, rate, payment terms, and accessorial conditions **[sourced]**. There is no federal form; each broker writes its own **[sourced]**. The signed rate confirmation is the document that makes an accessorial enforceable **[sourced]**. | Not a charge |
| **Broker-carrier agreement (BCA)** | The master agreement governing the ongoing broker-carrier relationship, incorporated by reference into each rate confirmation **[sourced]**. The BCA is where the 30 to 90 day claims submission window is usually written **[sourced]**. | Not a charge |
| **Bill of lading (BOL)** | The document issued at pickup that is simultaneously a receipt for the goods, the contract of carriage, and (in its original form) a document of title **[sourced]**. Travels with the freight. | Not a charge |
| **Proof of delivery (POD)** | The record confirming the goods reached the intended recipient, who received them, when, and in what condition **[sourced]**. Usually a signature, increasingly a photo plus timestamp plus GPS **[sourced]**. The signed delivery copy of the BOL often serves as the POD **[sourced]**. | Not a charge |
| **Requested amount** | The amount the carrier asks for on a claim. In the app it is a required field, typed in by the user; the app does not calculate it from event times and rates. | Per claim |
| **Accepted amount** | The amount the broker agreed to pay. Required when a claim moves to `accepted` or `partially_accepted`. For partial acceptance it must be less than the requested amount. | Per claim |
| **Recovered amount** | The cash actually received. Recorded separately from the accepted amount when a payment is recorded, and never defaulted, so short payment is visible. | Per claim |
| **Payment terms / net 30** | When the broker pays after receiving the invoice and POD. Standard pay is commonly net 30 **[sourced]**; quick pay is faster payment for a percentage fee **[sourced]**. | Per rate confirmation |
| **Factoring** | Selling invoices to a factoring company for immediate cash at a discount. Trade guidance notes accessorials should be billed on the same invoice as the load so the factor funds them together **[sourced]**. ClaimTrail has no factoring integration. | Discount varies |
| **Surety bond claim** | A claim against the broker's $75,000 FMCSA surety bond, the escalation path when a broker refuses to pay charges on a signed rate confirmation **[sourced]**. ClaimTrail does not model bond claims. | $75,000 bond **[sourced]** |
| **Deadhead** | Miles driven empty, for example driving to a pickup for a load that then cancels. TONU partly compensates deadhead **[sourced]**. | Per rate confirmation |
| **FCFS (First Come, First Served)** | A pickup or delivery with no appointment time; the driver is worked in arrival order **[sourced]**. Matters for detention because the free-time clock has no appointment time to anchor to. | Not a charge |

### 5.1 Amounts: three numbers, not one

A claim in ClaimTrail carries three separate money fields, and the
distinction between them is the difference between paperwork and cash:

1. **Requested amount**: what the carrier asks for. Set at claim
   creation, typed in by hand.
2. **Accepted amount**: what the broker agreed to pay. Set when the
   claim moves to `accepted` or `partially_accepted`.
3. **Recovered amount**: what actually arrived. Set when a payment is
   recorded, with a payment date and an optional payment reference.

The leakage math in v0.1.0 is the story of these three numbers
drifting apart: carriers bill 75% of detention owed, and only about
55% of billed invoices are paid, so roughly 41% of what is owed is
ever collected. ClaimTrail's job is to narrow each gap in turn: more
events turned into claims (requested), more claims accepted with proof
(accepted), more accepted claims actually paid (recovered).

## 6. Open questions only interviews can answer

These questions are unanswered by this document and by v0.1.0. They
are the interview script for v0.2.0. No answer is assumed here.

1. **Free time in practice.** Is 2 hours actually the free time on
   the rate confirmations your brokers send, or do your brokers
   commonly write 1 hour, 3 hours, or nothing at all? When does the
   clock start on your loads: appointment time, check-in, or dock
   assignment?
2. **Submission windows.** What submission deadline do your brokers
   enforce in practice: 24 to 48 hours, 30 days, 90 days, or no stated
   window? Have you ever had a claim denied purely for late filing,
   and how late was it?
3. **Notice requirements.** Do your brokers require you to notify
   them while the driver is still on site, before free time expires?
   What happens if you notify late or not at all?
4. **Evidence that actually wins.** Which documents have your brokers
   accepted, and which have they rejected? Is a timestamped photo
   enough, or do they insist on signed in/out times on the BOL? Do
   they accept ELD or GPS data?
5. **Partial acceptance.** How often do brokers partially accept, and
   on what grounds: fewer hours, a lower rate, or a flat cap? Do you
   negotiate the partial amount or take what is offered?
6. **Silence.** What share of submitted claims simply never get a
   reply? How long do you wait before following up, and what does the
   follow-up look like?
7. **Payment timing.** How long after acceptance does payment actually
   arrive? Is it net 30 in practice, or longer? How often is the
   amount received less than the amount accepted, and why?
8. **Rate ranges.** Are the v0.1.0 ranges right for your lanes: $50
   to $75 per detention hour, $250 to $350 per layover day, $200 to
   $300 TONU, $150 to $250 lumper? Or do the wider 2026 trade-guide
   ranges ($50-$100 detention, $150-$300 layover, $150-$350 TONU,
   $25-$500 lumper) match your experience better?
9. **Events beyond the seven categories.** Do you regularly bill
   driver assist, liftgate, tarp, hazmat, residential, or redelivery?
   Would you want those as first-class event types, or is the custom
   event enough?
10. **Who does the work.** Who in your operation records events, who
    assembles claims, and who reviews them? Is it the dispatcher, the
    driver, the owner, or nobody? How many days after delivery does a
    claim usually get assembled?
11. **Relationship fear.** Are there brokers you deliberately do not
    bill, to protect the relationship? What would have to change for
    you to bill them?
12. **Current tools.** What do you use today: paper, spreadsheets, a
    TMS detention field, a driver phone app, your dispatcher? What
    does that tool fail at that makes you want something else?
13. **Deadline defaults.** If the app set your submission deadline
    automatically, what number of days would you want: measured from
    delivery, from the event, or from claim creation? Would one global
    number work, or does it vary by broker?
14. **The claim package.** Would you send a generated PDF claim
    package to a broker, or do brokers want the invoice and the
    documents separately, in their own format?

## 7. Gaps between the documented workflow and the app

Each gap states what the real world needs, what the app does today,
and what would close it. None of these is a verdict on the product;
several are deliberate scope limits. They are recorded here so that
later versions can decide on them with the facts in front of them.

1. **No submission channel.** The real workflow ends with the claim
   reaching the broker. In the app, `submitted` is a status a user
   records by hand; the PDF package is generated but never sent. The
   app cannot prove the broker received anything. Closing this needs
   an email send (to the billing address on the rate confirmation) or
   a broker portal integration, plus a delivery confirmation.
2. **No GPS or ELD time capture.** The strongest detention evidence
   is a timestamp the driver cannot argue with and the broker cannot
   dispute **[sourced]**. The app records arrival, departure, and
   event times as typed-in values. Closing this needs a mobile capture
   flow (driver taps arrival and departure, GPS stamped), which v0.1.0
   already identified as the most valuable future feature.
3. **No rate confirmation parsing.** The app stores the rate
   confirmation as a file and never reads its terms. It cannot warn
   that a rate confirmation has no detention clause, cannot pre-fill
   the detention rate or free time, and cannot calculate the requested
   amount from event times. The requested amount is typed in by hand,
   which is where arithmetic errors enter.
4. **Deadline clock starts at claim creation, not delivery.** The
   real-world clock runs from delivery (or the event). The app adds
   the configured days to the moment the claim was created. A claim
   assembled a week after delivery silently gets a week of extra time.
   Closing this needs the deadline basis to be the event date or the
   delivery date, configurable per organization.
5. **No deadline when no setting is configured.** An organization
   that never configures a deadline setting gets no deadline rows at
   all, and nothing warns anyone. A claim can sit unsubmitted forever
   with no `overdue` state. Closing this needs a shipped default
   (for example, 30 days from delivery, labelled as a default to be
   confirmed) or a warning on claims without deadlines.
6. **No default evidence requirements.** The app ships no seeded
   requirement set, so readiness reports 100% and `ready` for a claim
   with no evidence attached. A new organization sees every claim as
   ready until someone configures requirements. Closing this needs a
   seeded default set, for which the matrix in §3.2 is the draft.
7. **Readiness counts files, not quality.** One file per required
   category makes a claim "ready", whatever the file contains. The
   code is honest about this (the disclaimer is in the service and in
   the API response), but the UI must carry the same wording or users
   will read "ready" as "will be accepted".
8. **Accepted but unpaid has no state.** Once a claim is accepted,
   the only way forward is recording a payment. There is no follow-up
   schedule, no payment-overdue state, and no escalation path. Given
   that fewer than half of billed detention charges are paid (v0.1.0),
   the accepted-to-paid gap is where a large share of the money is,
   and the app currently has no workflow for it.
9. **No shipment cancellation state.** A TONU event means a load was
   cancelled after dispatch, but shipments have no cancelled status:
   the lifecycle is draft, planned, in transit, delivered, completed,
   archived, forward only. A cancelled load's shipment record has no
   honest final state.
10. **One claim, one event.** A claim links to at most one shipment
    event. A load with detention at both pickup and delivery needs two
    claims, or one claim with the second event unlinked. The real
    world bills both on one invoice.
11. **Evidence attaches to claims, not events.** A driver photograph
    taken at the gate has nowhere to live until the claim is created,
    which may be days later. The evidence trail starts at claim
    assembly instead of at the event.
12. **A single file cannot serve two categories.** A signed BOL that
    is also the POD must be uploaded twice or one role goes
    unrecorded, because each evidence file carries exactly one
    category.
13. **No broker response tracking beyond the status.** The app records
    the outcome (accepted, partially accepted, rejected with a reason)
    but not the conversation: no counter-offer, no questions from the
    broker, no record of silence. Broker communication exists only as
    manually uploaded files.
14. **Submission cannot be corrected.** After submission the claim
    cannot return to review. A mistake found after submission can only
    be fixed by waiting for rejection and then reworking. In the real
    world a carrier sends a corrected invoice.

## 8. Validation status

- **What this document validates.** The workflow stages, the claim
  lifecycle, the evidence matrix, the deadline rules, and the glossary
  are validated against two things: the ClaimTrail code as it exists
  today, and public trade sources accessed 2026-11-22. Where code and
  practice differ, the difference is stated as a gap in §7, not
  smoothed over.

  carriers and real brokers: free-time practice, submission windows,
  notice requirements, which evidence wins, partial-acceptance
  patterns, silence rates, payment timing, rate ranges, and whether
  anyone will use a standalone claim tool instead of their TMS field
  or driver app. No interviews have been conducted. No pilot customers
  exist. No broker has confirmed any part of this workflow.

  questions in §6 to real carriers and dispatch agencies. The evidence
  matrix in §3.2, the rate ranges in §5, and the deadline defaults
  assumed in §4 are the hypotheses those interviews test. Until then,
  treat every [hypothesis] label in this document as exactly that.

  checklist items: shipment-to-claim workflow (§1), claim lifecycle
  (§2), evidence requirements (§3), deadline handling (§4), financial
  terminology (§5).

## 10. Limitations of this document

- No interviews, no pilot customers, no primary data. Every conclusion
  about broker practice, submission windows, and evidence standards is
  drawn from trade guides and vendor pages, which describe common
  practice, not any specific broker's rules.
- Rate ranges differ between sources (see the flagged differences in
  §5). The v0.1.0 ranges are kept as the reference figures; the wider
  2026 trade-guide ranges are flagged for interview testing, not
  adopted.
- The app behaviour described here is the code as read on 2026-11-22.
  The app has not been run end to end for this document, and live
  PostgreSQL, Redis, and Docker verification remain outstanding for
  the product as a whole.
- This document is not legal advice. Rate confirmations and
  broker-carrier agreements are contracts; where a term is unclear or
  unusual, a carrier should get professional advice before relying on
  it.
