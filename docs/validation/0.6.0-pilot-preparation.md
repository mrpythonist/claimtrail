# ClaimTrail v0.6.0: Pilot Preparation

Phase 0, Business Validation. Written 2026-11-25. This is the last
Phase 0 version in the master checklist, and it is a preparation
version, not a launch version. It identifies who the pilot customers
would be, writes the onboarding runbook they would follow, prepares
the sample data a pilot would start from, and defines the metrics
that would decide whether the pilot worked. It does not recruit a
pilot customer, run a pilot, or report pilot results.

Read this document with that limit in mind. Every figure in it is
labelled **[sourced]** (from the cited source) or **[estimate]**
(ClaimTrail's own arithmetic on sourced inputs, a hypothesis until
interviews or a pilot validate it). No pilot customer has been
recruited. No carrier or dispatch agency has been spoken to. No
interview has been conducted. The profiles in section 2 are
descriptions of the kind of customer to look for, not descriptions
of real companies: they carry no names, no quotes, no commitments
and no contact details, because inventing any of those would be
fabrication. Recruitment needs Aqib's outreach, and that is the
v0.2.0-dependent step: interviews stay deferred per Aqib's decision
of 2026-11-21, and the pilot cannot start until that decision is
revisited. Section 3 gives him the shortlist process instead of a
fake shortlist.

This version answers the four v0.6.0 checklist items and the one open
question v0.5.0 left for it: re-check Triporto and DetentionHero
before v0.6.0. It also records one new competitor found in that
pass (DetentionDesk) and carries the pilot measurement targets from
v0.5.0 into a full metric set.

## 1. What a pilot is for, and what it is not

A pilot is a small, supervised, time-boxed trial with real customers
doing real work on real loads. It is not a beta with whoever signs
up, not a free tier with a different name, and not a demo. The
differences matter because each one changes what the pilot can prove.

A beta tests whether the software works. ClaimTrail already has that
evidence from its own test suites (382 backend tests and 241
frontend tests passing at v1.1.3, plus live smoke tests). A pilot
tests whether the work changes: whether a dispatcher who used to
chase detention by phone and spreadsheet now files more claims, files
them faster, and collects more of them, and whether they will pay for
that change when the trial ends. Only people whose money is on the
line can answer that.

A free tier tests whether people will use something that costs
nothing. A pilot tests whether people will pay $19 to $149 a month,
after using it for 30 days on real loads. The v0.5.0 trial design
(30 days, no card, full Growth tier) is the pilot's entry offer for
exactly that reason: the pilot customer experiences the outcome, the
claim paid, before being asked to pay. If the pilot runs on the
narrow free tier instead, it measures the wrong thing, because a
customer on 5 claims a month with no PDF export cannot see one
claim through to payment.

A demo tests whether the workflow can be shown. The sample datasets
in section 5 and `pilot/pilot-scenarios.md` are a demo: twelve
synthetic shipments walked end to end in under an hour. A demo proves
the screens work in sequence. It proves nothing about whether a
dispatcher will open the product on a Tuesday morning when three
drivers are waiting at a dock and a broker is not answering the
phone. The pilot is the test of that Tuesday morning.

The pilot has three jobs, in order:

1. **Activation.** Does a real customer get from signup to first
   claim submitted inside the trial, without Aqib doing it for them?
   If activation fails, nothing else in the pilot matters, because a
   customer who never files a claim cannot tell you whether claims
   are worth paying for.
2. **Outcome.** Do the pilot's claims get accepted and paid at a rate
   that makes the subscription look cheap? The v0.5.0 value math says
   one collected claim of $120 pays for about 2.4 months of Growth at
   $49 per month [estimate]. The pilot tests that sentence against
   real brokers and real payment behaviour. Fewer than 50 percent of
   billed detention invoices are paid today [sourced] (ATRI 2024, via
   v0.1.0), so the pilot must expect rejections and silence, and
   measure the collection rate honestly rather than reporting the
   requested total as if it were revenue.
3. **Conversion.** At day 30, does the pilot customer pay? The
   targets are the v0.5.0 ones: 15 percent trial-to-paid as good,
   8 percent as the floor; 3 to 5 percent free-to-paid as good.
   Those are targets for measurement, not promises, and section 6
   turns them into decision rules.

What the pilot is not: it is not validation of the beachhead. Three
pilot customers, however good, cannot validate a market of 67,000
small fleets and an uncounted number of dispatch agencies [sourced]
(v0.1.0). They can invalidate a bad assumption fast: if three
well-chosen pilots all refuse to pay, that is a finding. If one pays
and two do not, that is a question. A negative result is readable as
a result, not as a failure to run the pilot properly.

Two scope decisions shape what follows. First, the pilot
is 30 days per customer, not longer, because the trial it measures
is 30 days (v0.5.0 section 7). A longer pilot would measure a
different offer. Second, the pilot is capped at three customers in
the first cohort: small enough for Aqib to support personally, and
large enough to cover the three profiles in section 2, one each. A
fourth pilot before the first three have reached day 30 would spread
support thinner without adding a new profile, so it waits for the
cohort review.

## 2. Pilot profiles: who to look for

The profiles come from the v0.1.0 beachhead: dispatch agencies and
2 to 20 truck fleets running reefer or spot freight. Each profile
states the fit criteria, what the pilot learns from that profile
that the other two cannot teach, and where to find candidates. The
profiles are ordered by learning value, not by ease of recruitment.

A candidate who fits none of the three profiles is not a pilot
customer, however enthusiastic they are: a 50-truck fleet on a full
TMS will compare ClaimTrail to its TMS and find it thin; a driver
with one truck and no office will compare it to a $2.92 per month
phone app and find it expensive. Both comparisons are fair, and both
are the wrong test. The scorecard in section 3 exists to say no to
those candidates politely.

### 2.1 Profile A: the dispatch agency (the beachhead test)

**The profile.** A dispatch service running back-office dispatch for
several small carriers, typically 5 to 20 carriers, each running 1 to
10 trucks, with the agency handling rate confirmations, check calls,
invoicing and detention chasing as part of its service. The agency
charges the carrier a percentage of gross linehaul, commonly 5 to 8
percent [sourced] (v0.1.0; confirmed in v0.5.0), and detention
chasing is included in that fee by the services that do it. The
agency has one or two dispatchers and an owner who does billing, and
it runs on phone, email, spreadsheets and a load board, not on a TMS.

**Fit criteria.** All of these must be true:

- The agency manages claims or detention billing for at least 3
  carriers today, and can name them (to Aqib, in confidence).
- It bills detention today, however badly: by phone, by email, on
  paper, or through a driver app. An agency that never bills
  detention cannot test a billing tool.
- It has someone whose job already includes chasing detention. That
  person is the pilot user, and they must be willing to use
  ClaimTrail for real loads during the pilot, not just look at it.
- It is willing to run each carrier as a separate organization in
  ClaimTrail, because that is how multi-tenancy works today, and to
  say plainly whether that is workable or a burden. The agency
  dashboard is a v2.1.0 feature, not a pilot feature.
- It will share outcome data: how many claims were filed, accepted,
  rejected and paid, and why the rejected ones were rejected. Without
  that, the pilot measures activity, not outcome.

**What the pilot learns from this profile.** Whether the agency desk
is real. v0.4.0 scored it at 7 to 8 out of 10 as an unvalidated
estimate, the strongest differentiation in the field, because no
rival offers multi-tenant operations. The pilot tests whether one
account running many carriers' claims is worth $149 per month base
plus $12 per extra carrier (v0.5.0 section 8), whether the
per-carrier organization model works at 5 to 20 carriers, and
whether the agency pays per carrier or expects a flat fee. It also
tests the hardest workflow question: the dispatcher chases drivers
for times by phone, because ClaimTrail has no driver capture flow
(v0.4.0 gap 3).

**Sourcing channels.** Dispatch agencies advertise, which is one
reason they are the beachhead. In the order to try them: dispatch
directories and listings where agencies advertise detention chasing;
agency websites that list detention, layover and TONU as services;
trucking forums and Facebook groups where small carriers recommend
dispatchers by name; referrals from the other two pilot profiles
(the warmest channel once profile B or C is recruited); and direct
outreach from Aqib's own mailbox, not a cold-email tool. The pilot
ask is a partnership, not a sales email: what the pilot involves,
what it costs (nothing for 30 days), what Aqib asks in return (30
minutes a week and honest outcome data), and what happens at day 30.

**Disqualifiers.** Already on a TMS with a detention workflow;
charges carriers separately for detention recovery; wants a
white-label product (not offered); will not name the carriers it
serves.

### 2.2 Profile B: the 5 to 10 truck reefer fleet on spot freight (the value test)

**The profile.** A carrier running 5 to 10 trucks, reefer or
refrigerated freight, mostly spot-market loads, with a dispatcher or
office person who handles paperwork and one owner who makes buying
decisions. Reefer is the highest-detention segment in the ATRI data:
detained at 56.2 percent of stops, against 42.5 percent for spot
fleets generally and 32.6 percent for truckload dry van [sourced]
(v0.1.0). A 5 to 10 truck reefer fleet on spot freight therefore has
the highest recoverable value per truck in the beachhead.

**Fit criteria.** All of these must be true:

- Fleet size 5 to 10 trucks, with at least half the loads reefer or
  spot-market. A 5-truck dry-van fleet on contract freight has a
  different detention pattern and belongs in profile C's orbit, not
  here.
- The carrier bills detention today, at least sometimes, and can
  say what share of what it bills it collects.
- There is a named dispatcher or office person who will be the daily
  user, and an owner who will make the day-30 decision. If the same
  person is both, that is fine, but the pilot needs both roles
  covered, because a dispatcher who loves the tool and an owner who
  will not pay for it is a failed pilot.
- The carrier is not on a full TMS with a detention workflow
  (Tezify, TenTrucks, Eden One or equivalent). A carrier already
  paying $145 or more a month for a TMS has little reason to add a
  standalone tool, and the pilot would measure TMS overlap, not
  ClaimTrail's value.
- The carrier runs loads through brokers who pay on net 30 or
  similar terms, so at least some pilot claims can reach a payment
  decision inside the 30-day window or shortly after it.

**What the pilot learns from this profile.** Whether the value math
holds. A 5-truck carrier has on the order of $30,000 a year of
uncollected detention value on the table [estimate] (v0.1.0 section
1.3), and one collected claim of $120 pays for about 2.4 months of
Growth [estimate] (v0.5.0 section 3.2). Profile B tests those two
sentences with real numbers: how many detention events the fleet
has in 30 days, how many become claims, how many are accepted and
paid, and what the owner says when shown the recovered total against
the $49 per month Growth price. It also tests the Growth tier's fit:
3 to 10 trucks, 100 claims a month, 10 members. If a 6-truck fleet
files 40 claims in a month, the tier limits are wrong; if it files
4, the price is the whole question.

**Sourcing channels.** Small fleets are harder to reach than
agencies because they do not advertise a service; they advertise
for drivers. In the order to try them: trucking job boards and
driver-hiring posts that name fleet size and freight type; FMCSA
carrier census data and carrier directories filtered by fleet size,
checked against the carrier's own website for an office contact (do
not scrape or buy contact data; use public listings); reefer and
spot-freight communities where small carriers discuss detention and
brokers by name; referrals from profile A; and Aqib's own network
and the ColdReach leads process, where the pilot ask is raised as
its own conversation, never mixed into a job application.

**Disqualifiers.** Fewer than 5 or more than 10 trucks; contract
freight with detention handled by the shipper; bills detention
through a factoring company and will not change that flow; owner
will not commit 30 minutes a week to the pilot.

### 2.3 Profile C: the 2 to 3 truck owner-operator or small fleet that bills detention regularly (the price test)

**The profile.** An owner-operator or 2 to 3 truck fleet where the
owner drives or dispatches, bills detention regularly (at least a few
claims a month), and does the paperwork themselves or with one
helper. This is the Starter tier buyer: 1 to 2 trucks at $19 per
month, or a 3-truck fleet at the bottom of Growth at $49 per month.
It is the most price-sensitive profile and the closest to the driver
app anchor: Detention Buddy at $2.92 per month and Run Your Rig at
$9.99 per driver per month [sourced] (v0.4.0). If this profile will
pay $19 a month, the price wedge is real; if they will only pay
$10, the success-fee plan becomes the primary model (v0.5.0
section 10.2).

**Fit criteria.** All of these must be true:

- Fleet size 2 to 3 trucks. A 1-truck owner-operator is a later
  volume play, not a pilot customer: ClaimTrail today is a desktop
  web app, and a single driver with no office is better served by a
  phone app (v0.1.0 section 3.3).
- Bills detention at least 3 times a month today. Below that, the
  pilot cannot generate enough claims in 30 days to measure
  anything.
- Does the paperwork on a computer, not only on a phone. The pilot
  needs a user who will open a web app daily, and a phone-only
  operator will churn on the form factor rather than the product.
- Willing to import or enter real shipment data and to use the
  sample CSV format as the import template.
- Willing to answer the pricing questions honestly at day 30,
  including the success-fee question: $49 a month flat, or $0 plus
  15 percent of what is actually collected, capped at $49 a month
  (v0.5.0 section 6).

**What the pilot learns from this profile.** Whether $19 to $49 a
month is payable at all. The desk tiers are mispriced if most
interviewees will not pay more than $10 a month for any detention
software (v0.5.0 section 10.2), and profile C is where that test
lands hardest, because the buyer compares ClaimTrail to a $2.92
phone app, not to a $99 desk rival. It also tests onboarding at its
worst: the owner has no IT staff, no dispatcher to delegate to, and
no patience for a setup that takes a day. If profile C activates
inside week 1 (section 4.3), the onboarding works. It also tests
the free tier from the other direction: a 2-truck fleet filing 4
claims a month fits inside the free tier's 5-claim cap, so the pilot
must watch whether they would stay on free forever.

**Sourcing channels.** Owner-operators and micro-fleets are the
largest pool (about 1.02 million carriers at 5 trucks or fewer, 92
percent of authorized-for-hire carriers [sourced], v0.1.0) and the
hardest to reach individually. In the order to try them:
owner-operator forums, Facebook groups and YouTube comment threads
where small fleets discuss detention, brokers and billing;
load-board communities and rate-discussion threads where
owner-operators compare detention pay by broker; referrals from
profiles A and B; and Aqib's own network. Do not buy lists, do not
scrape phone numbers, and do not message drivers while they are on
the road. The pilot ask goes to the owner at their office contact,
in writing, with time to consider it.

**Disqualifiers.** One truck only; bills detention less than 3 times
a month; phone-only paperwork; happy with a driver app and will not
run a second tool alongside it for 30 days; unwilling to share
outcome data.

## 3. Shortlist selection: the scorecard and the outreach process

No names are listed here, because no candidates have been identified
and listing invented ones would be fabrication. What follows is the
process Aqib runs when he is ready, and the scorecard he scores each
candidate against. A candidate needs 12 points or more out of 20 for
a pilot place, and must pass every hard gate regardless of score.

### 3.1 The scorecard

Score each candidate 0 to 4 on each of five factors. The factors are
weighted by what the pilot needs to learn, not by how easy the
candidate is to recruit.

| Factor | 0 | 2 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Profile fit | Fits no profile, or fits only loosely | Fits one profile on most criteria | Fits one profile on every criterion in section 2 |
| Bills detention today | Never bills, or will not say | Bills sometimes, irregularly | Bills regularly and can say what share is collected |
| Named daily user | No one named; "someone will do it" | A user named but not yet committed | A named user who has agreed to use it daily for real loads |
| Decision maker engaged | Owner not involved | Owner aware, not committed | Owner has agreed to the day-30 decision meeting |
| Outcome data willingness | Will not share results | Will share counts only | Will share counts, amounts, rejection reasons and payment timing |

Hard gates (any one fails, the candidate is out regardless of score):

- The candidate is on a full TMS with a detention workflow and will
  not run ClaimTrail alongside it.
- The candidate will not use real loads during the pilot.
- The candidate will not share outcome data at all.
- The candidate wants a white-label product, a custom feature, or a
  price that does not exist.
- The candidate has no verifiable business presence.

Scoring notes: score honestly, not hopefully. A candidate who scores
4 on profile fit and 0 on decision-maker engagement is a 4-point
candidate with a missing owner, not a 12-point candidate with a
minor gap. The day-30 decision cannot happen without the owner.
Record the score and the reason for each factor in one line per
candidate, in the pilot notes, so the decision to offer or decline
a place can be checked later. Do not keep the scores in this
document; they belong in Aqib's working notes, because they will
contain real names and contact details.

### 3.2 The outreach shortlist process

Step 1, build the list (Aqib, before any contact). Using the
sourcing channels in section 2, build a list of 15 to 20 candidates
across the three profiles: roughly 5 agencies, 5 to 7 fleets of 5 to
10 trucks, and 5 to 8 small fleets of 2 to 3 trucks. Fifteen to
twenty candidates for 3 places allows for non-replies and declines
without lowering the bar. Fewer than 10 means the bar will be
lowered to fill the places; more than 20 means the list was built by
volume rather than by fit.

Step 2, score before contact. Score each candidate on the five
factors using only public information and what is already known.
Unscorable factors are 0 until contact, not 2 as a placeholder.
Rank the list. Contact the top 3 per profile first.

Step 3, the pilot ask (Aqib, in writing, from his own mailbox).
One short message per candidate, plain language, no marketing
phrases. It states: what ClaimTrail is in one sentence; what the
pilot involves (30 days, free, their real loads, about 30 minutes a
week of their time plus daily use by the named user); what Aqib does
(support cadence in section 4.4); what happens at day 30 (subscribe,
switch to the success-fee plan, drop to the free tier, or leave,
with their data exportable); and what Aqib asks in return (outcome
data and an honest answer at day 30). It does not promise that
claims will be paid or that the product will save a named amount. It
does not offer a discount, a lifetime deal, or a custom feature. One
follow-up after a week of silence, then stop. A candidate who does
not reply is not a pilot customer.

Step 4, the qualification call (Aqib, 30 minutes). The call checks
the fit criteria in section 2 and the hard gates, and asks the
v0.5.0 pricing questions in interview form, because the pilot call
is also the first interview. If the candidate fails a hard gate, say so plainly and
thank them. If they pass, agree the named daily user, the owner
decision meeting at day 30, and the start date.

Step 5, offer the place. Three places, one per profile, offered in
profile order (agency first, because it is hardest to replace if it
drops out). If a profile has no candidate at 12 points or more,
leave the place empty rather than filling it with a lower-scoring
candidate from another profile. An empty place is honest; a
wrong-profile pilot teaches the wrong lesson. A profile with no
qualified candidate after 20 contacts is itself a finding: it says
the sourcing channel is wrong or the profile is not reachable, and
section 6 records that as a pilot-specific kill criterion.

Step 6, record the cohort. For each pilot, record the profile, the
score, the start date, the tier (Growth trial for all three), and the
named user and owner, in Aqib's working notes, not in this document.

## 4. Onboarding runbook

This is the runbook Aqib follows with each pilot customer, in the
order the work happens. It is grounded in what the app actually does
today, not in an ideal onboarding flow. Where the app has a gap the
pilot will hit, the runbook says so and gives the workaround.

### 4.1 The app's actual onboarding, step by step

Guided onboarding in the app is seven steps, defined in
`backend/app/Services/OnboardingService.php` and derived live from
the organization's data: a step completes the moment the underlying
data exists. Only the dismissal is stored. The seven steps, in the
order a new team meets them, are:

1. **Create your organization.** The workspace for shipments,
   claims and evidence. Completes on signup; it is the starting
   point, not a task.
2. **Invite a team member.** Dispatchers and billing staff work in
   the same organization. Completes when a second member exists or
   an invitation has been sent, even before anyone accepts.
3. **Add your first shipment.** Record the load, broker,
   appointments and rate. Completes when a shipment exists.
4. **Record a detention or layover event.** Events are what a claim
   is built on. Completes when a shipment event exists.
5. **Upload supporting evidence.** Bills of lading, rate
   confirmations and photos prove the claim. Completes when an
   evidence file exists.
6. **Create your first claim.** Turn an event into a claim with a
   requested amount. Completes when a claim exists.
7. **Review a claim.** Check the evidence, leave a reviewer note,
   and mark it ready to submit. Completes when a claim has reached
   ready to submit or beyond, or when a reviewer has left a note on
   any claim.

The runbook follows this order exactly, because it is the order the
app rewards. Configuration goes where the pilot will actually do it:
right after the first claim, when the readiness check first shows
its value, and right before the first submission, when the deadline
first matters.

Three honest caveats about the onboarding as built. First, the steps
are derived from data, so a pilot who imports the sample CSV
completes steps 3 and 4 in one action without touching the shipment
form. The runbook therefore asks the pilot to create one shipment by
hand before importing. Second, the onboarding never asks for
deadline settings or evidence requirements, and the app ships no
defaults for either, so a pilot who follows only the seven steps
ends up with claims that have no deadlines and readiness at 100
percent with no evidence (v0.3.0 gaps 5 and 6). The runbook adds
both as explicit setup tasks in week 1. Third, the
onboarding has no step for submission, payment or closure, because
those happen outside the app's guided flow. The runbook covers them
in weeks 2 and 3 as coached tasks, not as checklist steps.

### 4.2 The trial the pilot runs on

Every pilot runs on the v0.5.0 trial offer: 30 days, no card
required, full Growth tier. Thirty days is long enough to build,
submit and follow up a claim, and possibly to see the first fast
payment. No card is the trust
position for a product with no brand yet in a segment that distrusts
card-upfront offers. Full Growth means the pilot tests the tier the
customer would actually buy: 3 to 10 trucks, 100 claims a month, 10
members, plus custom fields, evidence templates and the operations
dashboard. A pilot on a restricted tier would measure the
restriction, not the product.

Two rules. First, the trial starts on the day the
pilot's first real shipment is in the system, not on the day the
account is created. Second, the trial ends with a choice, made in a
meeting, not in an email: subscribe to a paid tier, switch to the
success-fee plan ($0 a month base, 15 percent of amounts recorded as
paid, capped at $49 a month for the first year), drop to the narrow
free tier (1 truck, 5 claims a month, 30 days of history, no PDF
package export), or leave. Leaving means the data stays exportable
and nothing is deleted on downgrade or cancellation, which must be
stated to the pilot up front.

### 4.3 Week-by-week pilot plan

**Week 1: setup and import.** Goal: the pilot can create a shipment,
record an event, attach evidence and create a claim without help.
Day 1 is a 60-minute setup call: create the organization, invite the
named user, create one shipment by hand (a real recent load), record
its event, upload its evidence, create the claim, and review it to
ready to submit. That single load walks all seven onboarding steps
in one sitting.
Days 2 to 3: the pilot imports their recent shipments with the CSV
import, using `pilot/sample-shipments.csv` as the format template
and the column guide in section 5. Aqib checks the import preview
with them on a short call if the first import reports invalid or
duplicate rows. Days 4 to 5: configure the two
settings the app does not prompt for, evidence requirements per
event type (from the v0.3.0 matrix) and a deadline setting (30 days
from claim creation as the default). End-of-week check: at least 3
real shipments, at least 1 real event, at least 1 real claim, and
both settings configured. If the pilot has no claim by the end of
week 1, Aqib says so on the week 1 call rather than waiting for
week 2 to reveal it.

**Week 2: first claims.** Goal: the pilot files claims on their own,
at their own pace, for real loads. The pilot works their current
week's loads through the workflow: events recorded the same day or
the day after delivery (evidence is weakest when created days later,
v0.3.0 section 1.1), claims assembled within 24 to 48 hours of
delivery, evidence attached before review, readiness checked before
marking ready to submit. Aqib's role this week is review, not data
entry: he checks the readiness of the pilot's claims once mid-week
and flags any claim marked ready with no evidence, because that is
the app's known failure mode (readiness counts files, not quality,
v0.3.0 gap 7). End-of-week check: at least 5 claims created, at
least 2 marked ready to submit. The week 2 call asks: how long
does one claim take, from event to ready to submit, and is that
faster or slower than how they did it before?

**Week 3: submission and follow-up.** Goal: claims reach brokers.
The pilot generates the PDF claim package for each ready claim and
sends it to the broker themselves, by email to the billing address
on the rate confirmation, because the app does not send anything
(v0.3.0 gap 1). They record the claim as submitted in ClaimTrail
when they send it. Then
they follow up: the app has no follow-up schedule after submission
and no record of broker silence (v0.3.0 gaps 8 and 13), so the
runbook supplies the schedule the app lacks: follow up at 48 to 72
hours if unpaid, then escalate to broker management. The pilot keeps
a simple log outside the app: claim reference, date sent, broker,
follow-up date, response. Aqib collects that log at the week 3
call. End-of-week check: at least 3 claims submitted to real
brokers, at least 1 follow-up sent, and the pilot can say which
claims are awaiting a response and how long each has waited.

**Week 4: review and decision.** Goal: the pilot and Aqib look at
the numbers together and decide. Days 1 to 3: the pilot records any
broker responses (accepted, partially accepted, rejected with a
reason) and any payments received, with recovered amounts recorded
separately from accepted amounts, so short payment is visible
(v0.3.0 section 5.1). Aqib pulls the reports (section 6) and
prepares the one-page pilot summary: claims by status; requested,
accepted and recovered totals; time to first claim; readiness at
submission. Day 4 or 5: the day-30 decision meeting (60 minutes,
owner and named user present). The agenda is fixed: what was
recovered, what it cost in time, what would have to change for the
price to feel cheap (v0.5.0 interview question 4), and the choice:
subscribe, success-fee plan, free tier, or leave. The decision is
recorded with the reason, whatever it is. A pilot who leaves with a
stated reason is a successful pilot; a pilot who drifts past day 30
without a decision is a failed one, because the trial has become a
free tier by neglect.

### 4.4 Support cadence: what Aqib does each week

The pilot is a one-person support operation, so the cadence is
written to be sustainable for three pilots at once, and written down
so the pilot knows what to expect. A pilot who expects an answer in
an hour and gets one in a day feels ignored; one who was told a day
feels served.

- **Week 1:** one 60-minute setup call (day 1), one 30-minute
  check-in call (day 4 or 5), and message replies within one
  business day. Aqib checks the pilot's first import and first
  claim personally.
- **Week 2:** one 30-minute call mid-week, message replies within
  one business day. Aqib reviews claim readiness once mid-week.
- **Week 3:** one 30-minute call, message replies within one
  business day. Aqib collects the submission and follow-up log.
- **Week 4:** the 60-minute decision meeting, plus one short call
  earlier in the week to prepare the numbers. Message replies
  within one business day.
- **Response expectations, stated to the pilot up front:** messages
  answered within one business day; no phone support outside the
  scheduled calls; no weekend support; bugs that block claim
  creation get a same-day reply, everything else waits for the next
  scheduled call. This is a pilot, not a service contract.

If a pilot goes quiet for a full week (no logins, no claims, no
replies), Aqib sends one message asking whether to pause the trial
clock or end the pilot, and waits three business days. Silence after
that ends the pilot as a churn with reason "disengaged, no
response". A quiet pilot is not a patient pilot; it is a pilot that
has already ended.

### 4.5 Data migration: the sample CSV import

Real pilots do not start from empty screens. They start from their
own recent loads, and the fastest way to get those loads into
ClaimTrail is the CSV import (`backend/app/Services/
ShipmentCsvImporter.php`). The runbook's migration path is:

1. The pilot exports their recent loads from wherever they live
   today: a spreadsheet, a TMS export, or a dispatch sheet. If
   they have no list at all, they start by entering loads by hand
   and the import waits until week 2.
2. They reshape the export to the importer's columns, using
   `pilot/sample-shipments.csv` as the template. Required columns
   are `internal_reference` and `broker_or_customer_name`. Optional
   columns are `carrier`, `driver_name`, `driver_contact`, pickup
   and delivery locations and appointments, `actual_arrival_at`,
   `actual_departure_at`, `base_rate`, `currency`, `status` and
   `notes`. Headers match case-insensitively, and the importer
   accepts aliases such as "Broker", "Load ID" and "Rate". The
   sample file uses the canonical header names, and pilots should too.
3. Before importing, the pilot creates the carriers named in the
   `carrier` column, with the exact names, because the importer
   resolves carriers by name within the organization and rejects
   a row whose carrier does not exist yet. This is the most common
   first-import failure and the easiest to prevent.
4. The importer previews first: it parses and validates the file
   without creating anything, stores the parsed rows under a token
   for 30 minutes, and reports valid, invalid and duplicate rows.
   Only valid, non-duplicate rows are created on confirm, and a
   duplicate `internal_reference` is reported as a duplicate, not
   created twice. The file limit is 1,000 rows. Import only the
   last 30 to 60 days of loads: older history adds rows without
   adding pilot learning, and claims on loads older than the
   brokers' submission windows are dead claims.
5. Evidence does not migrate by CSV. Evidence files are uploaded
   against claims, not shipments, and there is no bulk evidence
   import. The pilot uploads evidence for the claims they actually
   file, not for every imported shipment.

What the import cannot do, stated plainly so the pilot is not
surprised: it cannot import events, claims, evidence or deadlines.
Those are created in the app after import, one by one, following
`pilot/pilot-scenarios.md`. It cannot import a cancelled load
honestly, because shipments have no cancelled status (v0.3.0 gap
9); a TONU load imports as `planned` and stays there. It cannot
resolve a carrier that does not exist, and it will not guess.

### 4.6 The exit and decision point at day 30

Day 30 is a meeting with four possible outcomes, and the runbook
treats all four as legitimate. The choice is the pilot's, and the
reason is recorded either way.

- **Subscribe.** The pilot picks a paid tier (Starter $19, Growth
  $49, Fleet $99, or Agency $149 base plus $12 per extra carrier,
  v0.5.0 section 8) and billing starts. Annual billing at 10 times
  monthly (two months free) is offered but not pushed; monthly,
  cancel any time, is the default in a churn-heavy segment.
- **Success-fee plan.** The pilot prefers $0 a month base plus 15
  percent of amounts recorded as paid, capped at $49 a month for
  the first year (v0.5.0 section 6). This is offered at the decision
  meeting, not before. Note the standing blocker: the plan needs
  metered billing in v1.2.0, which needs Stripe keys from Aqib, so
  a pilot who chooses it cannot be billed automatically on day 30.
  Until v1.2.0 ships, the success fee is invoiced manually, and the
  pilot must be told that.
- **Free tier.** The pilot drops to the narrow free tier (1 truck,
  5 claims a month, 30 days of history, no PDF export). This is a
  retention net, not a success: a pilot who drops to free has
  decided the product is worth keeping as a record but not worth
  paying for. Record the reason, and do not count them as a
  conversion.
- **Churn, with reason.** The pilot leaves. The reason is the most
  valuable sentence in the whole pilot: price, missing submission
  channel, missing GPS capture, too much typing, broker did not
  pay anyway, TMS already does it, owner said no. Record it
  verbatim where possible. Their data is exportable and nothing is
  deleted on cancellation.

## 5. Sample datasets

Two files, in `docs/validation/pilot/`:

- `sample-shipments.csv`: 12 realistic synthetic shipments across
  3 fictional carriers (Prairie Line Transport, Bluebonnet Freight,
  Copper State Carriers), covering detention, layover, TONU and
  lumper scenarios, using only headers the importer accepts. All
  12 rows are valid and importable: required columns present, dates
  well-formed, statuses within the accepted set (`completed` and
  `planned`), base rates numeric. Verified by parsing the file with
  python3 before this version was written. The file is clean on
  purpose: the importer's preview already reports duplicates and
  invalid rows, so the sample demonstrates the happy path, and the
  guide documents the duplicate and invalid handling in text rather
  than shipping a broken row a pilot might mistake for real data.
- `pilot-scenarios.md`: the companion guide. For each of the 12
  shipments it states which events and claims to create after
  import (event type, times, amounts, evidence items to attach),
  so a pilot user can walk the full shipment-to-claim workflow in
  under an hour. Event types and amounts use the v0.1.0 rate
  ranges: detention $50-75 per hour, layover $250-350 per day,
  TONU $200-300, lumper $150-250. The set totals $2,440 in
  requested amounts across 11 claims [estimate], with one event
  dismissed as a control row (a wait inside free time, nothing
  owed). The walkthrough ends with claims in mixed states (one paid,
  one partially accepted and paid, two submitted and awaiting a
  response, one rejected and reworked) to mirror the real world,
  where fewer than 50 percent of billed detention invoices are paid.

How to use them: for a demo, import the file into a throwaway
organization and follow the guide. For a real pilot, use the file
as the format template for the pilot's own loads (section 4.5) and
do not import the synthetic rows into the pilot's organization.
Demo data and pilot data must not mix. Delete the demo organization
before a real pilot starts.

## 6. Pilot success metrics

The measurement targets are the v0.5.0 ones, carried forward
unchanged: trial-to-paid 15 percent as a good result, 8 percent as
the floor; free-to-paid 3 to 5 percent as a good result. Around them
sit activation and outcome metrics, each with a target, a floor, a
measurement method naming the app surface that supplies the number,
and a decision rule. The methods name real endpoints, verified
against the code for this version: `GET /api/v1/reports/summary`
(counts by status and event type, six money figures per currency),
`GET /api/v1/reports/recovery-history` (recovered money per month
per currency) and `GET /api/v1/reports/export` (summary plus
per-claim rows as CSV), all in `ReportController.php`; lifecycle
timestamps are stamped by `ClaimTransitionService.php`, and
readiness comes from `ReadinessService.php`. Trial conversion is not
measured by the app today (billing is v1.2.0, still blocked on
Stripe keys), so it is measured from Aqib's pilot notes and the
day-30 decision record. A metric with no instrument is a metric
that will not be measured unless someone owns it, and Aqib owns it.

| Metric | Target (good) | Floor (no-go below) | Measurement method | Decision rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time to first claim | First claim created by day 3 | No claim by day 7 | Claim `created_at` vs trial start | Onboarding failed; fix it before recruiting more pilots |
| Activation: claims filed per org | 5+ claims by end of week 2 | Fewer than 3 claims by day 14 | `reports/summary` counts by status | Pilot is not doing real work; check whether the user or the workflow is the blocker |
| Evidence completeness at submission | 80%+ of submitted claims at readiness `ready` | Under 50% | Readiness status per claim at `ready_to_submit` | Evidence requirements or the readiness flow need work; do not blame the pilot |
| Submission rate | 70%+ of ready claims submitted within 48 hours | Under 40% | Claims reaching `submitted` vs `ready_to_submit` | The missing send channel is the blocker |
| Collection rate | 50%+ of submitted claims accepted (full or partial) | Under 25% | `reports/summary` by status | Evidence too weak or brokers do not pay; ask which before concluding |
| Recovered vs requested | Recovered 40%+ of requested on submitted claims | Under 15% | `reports/recovery-history`; recovered over requested | Value story fails; success-fee plan becomes the primary offer |
| Trial-to-paid conversion | 15% convert (good); cohort of 3 means at least 1 pays | 8% floor; cohort of 3 means 0 pay | Aqib's pilot notes and the day-30 decision record | 0 of 3 pay: no-go as priced; re-price or lead with the success fee |
| Free-to-paid conversion | 3 to 5% of free-tier orgs convert | Under 2% | Billing records once v1.2.0 ships | Measured after launch, not in the pilot |
| Retention at day 30 | Pilot still active and using the product at day 30 | Churned or disengaged before day 21 | Login and claim activity | Count as churn with reason, not a pending decision |
| Retention at day 60 | Paying customer still active at day 60 | Churned within 30 days of paying | Billing records once v1.2.0 ships | Post-pilot measure; a day-30 conversion that churns by day 60 converted the wrong customer |

Activation metrics are leading indicators and outcome metrics are
lagging ones. If activation
fails, the conclusion is about onboarding, not price. If activation
succeeds and collection fails, it is about evidence quality or
broker behaviour. If activation and collection succeed and
conversion fails, it is about price. The pilot is only useful if
the failure is attributed to the right one.

Cohort decision rules, applied when all three pilots reach day 30:

- **Go:** at least 2 of 3 pilots subscribe or choose the
  success-fee plan, activation targets met, and collection rate
  above 50 percent. Proceed to v1.2.0 billing with the v0.5.0
  tiers as priced.
- **Iterate:** 1 of 3 converts, activation met, but a single named
  gap (submission channel, GPS capture, evidence defaults) is
  cited by the non-converters. Fix that gap before the next
  cohort; do not re-price on one cohort.
- **No-go, re-price:** activation met, collection reasonable, but
  0 of 3 pay and the reason is price in every case. The v0.5.0
  kill criterion fires: the success-fee plan becomes the primary
  model and the subscription becomes the cap.
- **No-go, re-scope:** activation failed across the cohort, or the
  collection rate is under 25 percent with evidence complete. The
  problem is the product or the beachhead, not the price. Do not
  fix it by lowering the price.

### 6.1 Kill criteria carried forward from v0.5.0

These still apply, because no interview or pilot has tested them yet:

- If most pilots will not pay more than $10 a month for any
  detention software, the desk tiers are mispriced and the
  success-fee plan becomes the primary model.
- If most will only pay on recovery, the subscription launch is
  premature and the success-fee plan launches first.
- If agencies will not pay per carrier at any number, the agency
  beachhead is wrong and the $149 tier is dropped.
- If 30 days is not enough to judge the product and pilots need to
  see a claim paid, the trial must extend to the first paid claim
  rather than a fixed 30 days.
- If pilots already pay a TMS $145 or more a month and see no reason
  to add a standalone tool, the standalone buyer is narrower than
  the beachhead assumes.

### 6.2 Pilot-specific kill criteria

- **Zero pilots recruited by a stated date.** If 20 candidates are
  contacted and none scores 12 or more on the section 3 scorecard,
  or none accepts a pilot place, the beachhead sourcing is wrong,
  not the product. The product has not been tested, so it cannot
  be blamed. Set the date when outreach starts; a kill criterion
  without a date is a wish.
- **Zero activations.** If 3 pilots start and none files a claim
  by day 7, onboarding is broken. Stop recruiting and fix
  onboarding.
- **Zero submissions.** If pilots create claims but none is
  submitted to a broker, the manual submission step is the
  blocker, and the submission channel moves to the top of the
  roadmap before any further pilot cohort.
- **Zero payments with complete evidence.** If submitted claims
  with complete evidence are all rejected or ignored, check the
  rejection reasons before concluding anything: a broker rejecting
  for a missing detention clause on the rate confirmation is a
  different finding from a broker ignoring every claim.

## 7. Competitor re-check: Triporto and DetentionHero (2026-11-25)

Both home pages were opened in full on 2026-11-25, as v0.5.0

**Triporto (https://triporto.com/, opened 2026-11-25).** Still early
access. The page still says "Now accepting early access" and "Free
during beta. No credit card required." No price is published, no
paid plan exists, and the call to action is still a waitlist. The

detention timer, instant PDF invoices, one-tap email to the broker,

of it is shippable evidence: it is a landing page, and the fleet
dashboard remains the feature to watch, because if it ships, the
driver-app tier stops being cleanly separate from the small-fleet
desk. No change to the v0.4.0 assessment. Re-check again before the
pilot cohort starts, because a launch during the pilot would change
the competitive story the pilot is told.

**DetentionHero (https://detentionhero.com/, opened 2026-11-25).**
Still early access. The page is still an email capture ("Be one of
the first drivers with GPS-backed detention invoices"). No price is
published, no app-store listing was found in this pass, and the
page's figures are unchanged: $3.6B in detention pay lost annually,
63 percent of brokers denying claims citing no shipper-signed
in-time, and $4,200 average annual loss per owner-operator who stops
filing. Those figures are self-reported vendor claims with no
published method, as recorded in v0.5.0, and they are not cited as
facts anywhere else in this document. No change to the v0.4.0
assessment: a signal that the driver-app tier keeps attracting
entrants, not a live rival.

**One new find in this pass: DetentionDesk
(https://www.detentiondesk.com/pricing, opened 2026-11-25).**
A shipping product, not a waitlist, with a pricing model no rival
in v0.4.0 used: a single claim packet for $25 one-time, or monthly
access at $149 per month. The single packet buys one detention
calculation, one invoice, one claim editable until its first PDF is
generated, private evidence uploads with a checklist, follow-up and
payment-status tracking, and PDF-ready output. Monthly access adds
a detention timer, saved claim and evidence history, claim editing
and packet regeneration, and outstanding-detention tracking. Its
own comparison states monthly becomes cheaper at six packets in a
month ($150 against $149). Times are user-confirmed arrival and
departure with free time and a live estimate: no GPS or ELD
verification, stated plainly on the page. Evidence accepts PDF,
JPG, PNG, HEIC and HEIF up to 15 MB per file, and the page states
DetentionDesk does not verify or certify uploaded evidence. Payment
is by Stripe Checkout, cancellation is through the Stripe customer
portal, and the page states plainly that a purchase does not
guarantee a claim will be accepted or paid. Detention only: no
layover, TONU or lumper workflow is described. One account, team
seats not currently included. Two readings for ClaimTrail. First,
per-claim pricing now exists in the field at $25 a claim, a third
price anchor alongside the driver apps and the desk subscriptions:
a pilot customer filing 2 claims a month would pay $50 on
DetentionDesk's per-claim price against $49 for ClaimTrail Growth.
Second, DetentionDesk's $149 monthly price is the same number as
ClaimTrail's Agency base and DOCK2BILL Partner Pro, which is a
coincidence, not a benchmark, but it means $149 a month is now the
price of three different things. DetentionDesk goes on the watch
list for the next competitor pass; it does not change any v0.6.0
conclusion, because it is detention-only, single-account, and has no
claim lifecycle beyond status tracking.
